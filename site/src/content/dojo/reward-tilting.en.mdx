<Talk partner="nori" pose="question" id="log-base" label="What does the base contribute besides preference?" next="Try a seemingly reasonable objective">
“What if the scorer especially likes a bright white blob that is not a corgi? Do we want it to appear very often?”

We also want the original data to matter. Let $p_{\mathrm{data}}(x_0)$ denote the base distribution: probabilities for discrete results, densities for continuous images. It tells us where the original data places more weight. It is not another aesthetic score, and it does not guarantee every high-density result is good.

To combine original weights and reward in one objective, start with $\log p_{\mathrm{data}}(x_0)$. Log turns multiplication into addition; we will see how this connects to KL between distributions. With finite rewards, reweighting alone cannot create results outside the original support.

<Extra label="Why use log probability? Think through model training">
Switch briefly to training. We have independently drawn data $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ and change model parameters $\theta$. Here $i$ indexes data, not diffusion time. Likelihood and log-likelihood are

$$
\begin{gathered}
L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^n p_\theta(x_i),\\
\log L(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^n\log p_\theta(x_i).
\end{gathered}
$$

Log is strictly increasing, so taking the log of the same likelihood leaves its maximizing parameters unchanged. Replacing products of small numbers with sums also helps computation. Dividing by fixed $n$ preserves the maximizer too. Using the data average to estimate a population expectation gives $\mathbb E_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}[\log p_\theta(x_0)]$. When the relevant quantities are defined,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathbb E_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}[\log p_\theta]\\
&=\mathbb E_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}[\log p_{\mathrm{data}}]\\
&\quad-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\mathrm{data}}\|p_\theta).
\end{aligned}
$$

The data distribution is fixed, so maximizing average log-likelihood minimizes KL in this direction. Training fixes the data and changes the model. We will instead fix the base and choose a new sampling distribution $\widetilde p$. Keep these settings distinct.

Does “log preserves order” let us swap log and averaging? Suppose data probabilities for $A,B$ are $0.9,0.1$, while the model assigns $a,1-a$. Average probability is $0.9a+0.1(1-a)$, favoring $a=1$. Average log probability is

$$
0.9\log a+0.1\log(1-a).
$$

For $0<a<1$, its derivative is $0.9/a-0.1/(1-a)$, zero at $a=0.9$. The second derivative is negative, so this is the maximum. It retains the rarer $B$. **Taking a log after averaging and averaging logs optimize different objectives.**
</Extra>
</Talk>

<Reveal partner="mugi" pose="explain" id="collapse" label="Add the two results’ contributions" next="Check what the objective missed">
Both log base probabilities equal $\log(1/2)=-\log2$; only the reward differs. The new probabilities $\widetilde p(A),\widetilde p(B)$ sum to $1$, so the average score is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\widetilde p(A)(-\log2)\\
&+\widetilde p(B)(\lambda-\log2)\\
&=-\log2+\lambda\widetilde p(B).
\end{aligned}
$$

For any $\lambda>0$, assigning more probability to $B$ raises the average, eventually returning $B$ every time. Both images were plausible, but **this average score never requires retaining the other answer**.

<Extra label="Does this happen with more than two photos?">
For finitely many candidates, write each fixed image score as

$$
\begin{gathered}
F(x_0)=\lambda R(x_0)\\
+\log p_{\mathrm{data}}(x_0).
\end{gathered}
$$

The average $\sum_{x_0}\widetilde p(x_0)F(x_0)$ cannot exceed the largest $F$. Assigning all mass to a highest-scoring candidate attains that value. Ties allow mixing among maximizers, but still do not encourage retaining other answers.

Continuous spaces need more care: an ordinary density may not attain a point-concentrated maximum; increasingly concentrated sequences might only approach it. The shared problem is averaging fixed per-image scores without separately measuring the new distribution’s concentration.
</Extra>
</Reveal>

<Talk partner="lumi" pose="explain" id="entropy" label="How can we express avoiding concentration?" next="Try combining the three terms">
Entropy measures how spread out a distribution is: $H(\widetilde p)=-\mathbb E_{\widetilde p}[\log\widetilde p(x_0)]$. For our two photos, always choosing $B$ gives $0$, while equal probabilities give $\log2$. Adding entropy makes spreading probability across answers contribute too:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\max_{\widetilde p}\ \big\{\\
&\quad\lambda\mathbb E_{\widetilde p}[R(x_0)]\\
&+\mathbb E_{\widetilde p}[\log p_{\mathrm{data}}(x_0)]\\
&+H(\widetilde p)\big\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Every expectation averages over $x_0$ drawn from the new $\widetilde p$. The scoring program, base, and $\lambda$ stay fixed. We change the frequencies of results.
</Talk>

Substitute $p_{\mathrm{data}}e^{\lambda R}=Z_Rp_R$ into the log ratio. Separate the fixed $\log Z_R$. The remaining average of $\log(p_R/\widetilde p)$ is negative KL:

$$
\begin{gathered}

\right]\\

\end{gathered}
$$

<Talk partner="nori" pose="explain" id="same-objective" label="Read the objective back into the situation" next="Calculate the two-photo proportions">
“We now have new proportions that favor the user’s style while keeping the original distribution involved. Can ‘do not stray too far from the base’ appear directly in the objective?”

Yes. Entropy plus expected log base equals negative KL. Divide the objective by positive $\lambda$, which preserves its maximizer:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\max_{\widetilde p}\ \big\{\\
&\mathbb E_{\widetilde p}[R]\\
&-\tfrac1\lambda
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\widetilde p\|p_{\mathrm{data}})
\big\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

To see why, expand the KL definition:

$$
\begin{gathered}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\widetilde p\|p_{\mathrm{data}})\\
=\mathbb E_{\widetilde p}[\log\widetilde p-\log p_{\mathrm{data}}]\\
=-H(\widetilde p)\\
-\mathbb E_{\widetilde p}[\log p_{\mathrm{data}}].
\end{gathered}
$$

This KL direction differs from the earlier training example: now the expectation is over the $\widetilde p$ we choose.

A larger $\lambda$ makes the penalty coefficient for departing from the base smaller. We allow preference more influence, but cannot conclude that images become more plausible.

<Extra label="What if I specify a maximum departure instead of a penalty?">
We could maximize $\mathbb E_{\widetilde p}[R]$ subject to $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\widetilde p\|p_{\mathrm{data}})\le\delta$, where $\delta$ is the allowed departure.

With positive multiplier $1/\lambda$, the Lagrangian is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathbb E_{\widetilde p}[R]\\
&-\tfrac1\lambda\big[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\widetilde p\|p_{\mathrm{data}})-\delta
\big].
\end{aligned}
$$

The final $\delta/\lambda$ is constant in $\widetilde p$. For a fixed multiplier, this has the same optimizer as the KL-penalized objective. But arbitrary choices of $\delta,\lambda$ do not make the constrained and penalized problems equivalent.

For example, if $p_R$ has finite KL, choose $\delta=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_R\|p_{\mathrm{data}})$. Optimality of $p_R$ implies that any feasible $\widetilde p$ satisfies

$$
\begin{gathered}
\mathbb E_{\widetilde p}[R]\\
\le \mathbb E_{p_R}[R]\\
+\tfrac1\lambda\big[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\widetilde p\|p_{\mathrm{data}})-\delta\big]\\
\le\mathbb E_{p_R}[R].
\end{gathered}
$$

Thus $p_R$ also solves the constrained problem for this matched $\delta$. This is the reason the two forms connect.
</Extra>

<Extra label="Can reweighting describe recovering an original from a blurry photo?">
Suppose we observe $y$, such as an actually received blurry photo. Let $p(y\mid x_0)$ describe how likely an original image is to produce this observation, and choose $R(x_0)=\log p(y\mid x_0)$ and $\lambda=1$. Then

$$
\begin{gathered}
p_R(x_0)\\
\propto p_{\mathrm{data}}(x_0)p(y\mid x_0),\\
Z_R=\int p_{\mathrm{data}}(x_0)\\
\qquad\cdot p(y\mid x_0)\,\mathrm dx_0\\
=p(y).
\end{gathered}
$$

When $0<p(y)<\infty$, normalization gives the Bayes posterior $p(x_0\mid y)$. Here $y$ is an observation, unlike the desired condition “corgi.” It also differs from the base posterior given a sampling-time $x_t$ below. In this example, the observation enters as a log-likelihood reward.
</Extra>

<Extra label="Does preference learning for language models use this objective too?">
Fix a prompt, view $x_0$ as an answer, and let $p_{\mathrm{ref}}$ be the original answer distribution. A common preference objective is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\max_{\widetilde p}\ \big\{
&\mathbb E_{\widetilde p}[R]\\
&-\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\widetilde p\|p_{\mathrm{ref}})
\big\},
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $\beta>0$ controls retention of the reference. For this ideal distribution-level problem, $\lambda=1/\beta$ and optimal relative weights are $p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_0)e^{R(x_0)/\beta}$. This has the same structure as KL regularization in RLHF.

Actual language-model training usually updates policy parameters and faces capacity and optimization limits; some full objectives also include pretraining terms. This ideal optimal distribution is therefore not the exact outcome of every RLHF algorithm. Here we instead preserve generative parameters and try to change sampling.
</Extra>
</Talk>

<Talk partner="mugi" pose="suggest" id="p-plugin" study="plugin-plan" label="Mugi: what about estimating one clean image?" next="Put the proposal away and check what it omits">
“Including all possible sources sounds hard. We already have a denoiser. Could we denoise the current image, then give that to the aesthetic program?”

Recall $x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0+\sigma_t\epsilon$. Subtract predicted noise and divide out the signal scale:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\hat x_0(x_t,t)\\
=\frac{x_t-\sigma_t\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}
{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}.
\end{gathered}
$$

Here $\bar\alpha_t>0$ and $\sigma_t=\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$. Under ideal squared-error noise estimation, this equals the base posterior mean $\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]$; actual networks also have estimation error. It supplies a clean estimate, **not a uniquely recovered true source**.

<Reveal partner="nori" pose="explain" id="guidance" label="This step is an approximation" next="Check what the results let us claim">
The plug-in log is $\lambda R(\hat x_0)$, so the extra direction is approximated by

$$
\begin{gathered}
\nabla_{x_t}\log h_t(x_t)\\
\approx\lambda\nabla_{x_t}R(\hat x_0(x_t,t)).
\end{gathered}
$$

Write out the whole dependency using the chain rule:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\nabla_{x_t}R(\hat x_0(x_t,t))\\
=\left(\frac{\partial\hat x_0}{\partial x_t}\right)^{\!\top}
\nabla_{x_0}R(\hat x_0).
\end{gathered}
$$

The reward gradient describes changes to the clean estimate that increase its score. The Jacobian $\partial\hat x_0/\partial x_t$ describes how that estimate changes with the current noisy image. Their product converts the direction back to the $x_t$ we can modify. Fixed $\theta$ still allows this input gradient.

Our example already showed unequal weights, so this approximation cannot simply be called exact tilting. Even exact scores need the correct initial noisy distribution and reverse dynamics for actual sampling. Network estimation, finite noise, and a finite-step sampler add approximations to check.

<Extra label="Why check even the initial noise distribution?">
The same reweighting relation holds at starting time $T$:

$$
\begin{gathered}
p_T^R(x_T)\\
=\frac{p_T(x_T)h_T(x_T)}{Z_R}.
\end{gathered}
$$

If the forward process has completely forgotten the clean source, $x_T$ and $x_0$ are independent, so

$$
\begin{gathered}
h_T(x_T)=\mathbb E_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}[e^{\lambda R(x_0)}]\\
=Z_R.
\end{gathered}
$$

Then $p_T^R=p_T$, allowing the same base start. Finite noise need not achieve full independence. Even highly accurate scores do not establish exact sampling when started from a mismatched distribution.
</Extra>

<Extra label="Which approximations do practical methods address?">
A common practical route converts noisy images into denoised predictions, then uses an external differentiable request for a direction. <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.07121">Universal Guidance</a> discusses such use. Separating it from the exact posterior average shows where source uncertainty was omitted.

For example, <a href="https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/geyfman26a.html">Calibrated Test-Time Guidance</a> analyzes how common approximations bias Bayesian posterior sampling and proposes a consistent estimator under its conditions. <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.02884">Are We Really Tilting?</a> separates mode selection from excessive reward pursuit within modes in Gaussian and mixture examples, and studies reward damping. Read each result with its assumptions; do not extend it to arbitrary rewards and samplers.

Methods can improve posterior approximations, adjust guidance strength, or constrain updates. But a higher average reward checks only one quantity. Excessive preference strength may suppress plausible lower-scoring modes. Assess reward, plausibility, diversity, and whether the defined target is actually sampled separately.
</Extra>
</Reveal>
