<Talk partner="nori" pose="explain" id="helper" label="Can we find clues in the draft?" next="Ask a question it can answer">
Imagine drawing a few blurry lines. The animal looks vaguely like a dog, but its breed is unclear. A friend suggests, “Would shorter legs make it look more like a corgi?” We can try changing the draft rather than finishing the whole image and starting over.

In diffusion, our actual input is a noisy image $x_t$. The desired “corgi” is the condition $c$. Keep U1’s time convention: larger $t$ means more noise, and sampling moves toward clean images at $t=0$. The noisy image is formed as

$$
x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sigma_t\epsilon.
$$

Here $x_0$ is the original clean photo, $\bar\alpha_t$ is the retained signal fraction, $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ is added noise, and $\sigma_t=\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$ sets its amplitude. We therefore find a judgment model trained at different noise levels and ask it a class question.
</Talk>

<Say partner="nori" pose="explain" id="d-density" detour back="c-probability" label="Which comes first: image or breed?" next="Read the question again">
$p_t(x_t\mid c)$ means “specify corgis, then describe their distribution after adding noise.” By contrast, $p_t(c\mid x_t)$ means “see this image, then infer its class.” We gave the helper an image, so it directly answers the latter.

We want to generate the images described by the former. These quantities are related, but we need to derive that connection rather than swap the notation.
</Say>

<Reveal partner="lumi" pose="explain" id="bayes" label="How does judgment connect to generation?" next="Check what changes with the image">
Imagine selecting the corgi photos in the data, then adding noise with the original forward process. Their noisy density is $p_t(x_t\mid c)$, the distribution we want for this breed.

Yes, it is time for Bayes’ rule again:

$$
\begin{gathered}
p_t(x_t\mid c)\\
=\frac{p_t(x_t)\,p_t(c\mid x_t)}{p(c)}.
\end{gathered}
$$

The base $p_t(x_t)$ includes all kinds of images. Multiplying by the probability of “came from a corgi” gives relatively more weight to images likely to be corgis. The denominator $p(c)$ restores total mass to $1$. The helper’s judgment now connects to the images we want to generate.

<Extra label="How does the starting distribution enter this relationship?">
The $p_t(x_t\mid c)$ above is a whole family of noisy distributions: draw clean $x_0$ from the specified breed, then apply the same noising rule. Larger $t$ usually obscures more of the original image.

If $\bar\alpha_T$ is very small, conditional $x_T$ is also close to a standard Gaussian, so reverse sampling can start from approximate noise. Finite noising need not erase all breed information, however: $p_T(x_T\mid c)$ is not necessarily exactly Gaussian. The score identity, network estimation, starting approximation, and discretized sampler are separate steps.
</Extra>
</Reveal>

<Think partner="lumi" pose="question" id="t-direction" label="Turn density into a direction for change">
Having a density is not enough; we need a direction in which to change the image. Recall that a score differentiates log density with respect to the image. Taking logs first turns Bayes’ multiplication into addition:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\log p_t(x_t\mid c)\\
=\log p_t(x_t)\\
+\log p_t(c\mid x_t)\\
-\log p(c).
\end{gathered}
$$

Now differentiate with respect to $x_t$. The original-data term gives the base score; the fixed term has zero derivative. **Which term contributes the extra direction for corgis?** Try writing its gradient.
</Think>

<Reveal partner="lumi" pose="explain" id="direction" label="The extra direction from specifying a breed" next="Prepare the distribution shared by both routes">
Denote the conditional score by $s_t(x_t\mid c)$ and the base score by $s_t(x_t)$. Termwise differentiation gives

$$
\begin{gathered}
s_t(x_t\mid c)\\
=s_t(x_t)\\
+\nabla_{x_t}\log p_t(c\mid x_t).
\end{gathered}
$$

The last term points toward the fastest local increase in the log-probability of “came from a corgi” as we change $x_t$. **The directions add because Bayes’ product becomes a sum after taking logs.**

This is a relationship between exact densities and scores. In practice, networks estimate them for a reverse-time sampler. The score itself is not the distance to move the image. Differentiating away $p(c)$ also does not mean we have computed its value.
</Reveal>

<Talk partner="lumi" pose="explain" id="classifier-training" label="How should this helper practice?" next="Connect its judgment to the sampler">
“There are many levels of blur along the way. Let us add different amounts of noise to a breed-labeled photo. Even as the dog becomes hard to recognize, we still know its original breed, right?”

Let $\phi$ denote the helper’s parameters. Draw $(x_0,c)$, a noise level $t$, and Gaussian noise $\epsilon$, then form $x_t$ with the same noising rule. Ask it to assign probability to the correct breed. Its training loss is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathcal L_{\mathrm{cls}}(\phi)\\
&=\mathbb E_{(x_0,c),t,\epsilon}\big[\\
&-\log p_\phi(c\mid x_t,t)\big].
\end{aligned}
$$

Higher probability for the correct breed means smaller negative log-probability. This is the noisy classifier’s training exercise: directly noise training photos, without waiting for the generative model to draw them. At high noise, the photo may genuinely be unrecognizable, so a reasonable answer may be uncertain.

Training changes $\phi$ using $\nabla_\phi\mathcal L_{\mathrm{cls}}$. To help change an image now, fix $\phi$ and use $\nabla_{x_t}\log p_\phi(c\mid x_t,t)$. Both are gradients, but the object to be changed differs.
</Talk>

<Reveal partner="lumi" pose="explain" id="classifier-score" label="Put the needed directions together" next="Examine the limitations">
Estimate the base score with the trained model, then add the helper’s input gradient:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\widetilde s_\theta(x_t,t,c)\\
=s_\theta(x_t,t)\\
+w\nabla_{x_t}\log p_\phi(c\mid x_t,t).
\end{gathered}
$$

Here $w$ sets the suggestion’s strength. Starting from an unconditional base, $w=0$ retains the base; if both estimates are exact, $w=1$ gives the conditional score derived above. In practice, the reverse sampler uses this combined score to determine the next image update. This is Lumi’s classifier-guidance proposal.

<Extra label="What if the generative model already uses a condition?">
We started from an unconditional base. Adding classifier guidance to an already conditional score is **extra strengthening beyond the original request**; an extra coefficient of $0$ retains the conditional prediction. The two starting points cannot both call $w=1$ “no strengthening.” First identify the base answer, then inspect the extra term’s coefficient.
</Extra>
</Reveal>

<Think partner="lumi" pose="question" id="t-noise" label="What if the network predicts noise?">
U1’s network usually outputs $\epsilon_\theta$. At a noisy time, with noise amplitude $\sigma_t=\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}>0$, its score representation is

$$
s_\theta(x_t,t)=-\frac{\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}{\sigma_t}.
$$

First consider classifier guidance: add $w$ times the classifier’s input gradient to an unconditional base score. **What happens to the extra term’s sign and scale in noise-prediction form?** Multiply the whole score equation by $-\sigma_t$.
</Think>

<Reveal partner="lumi" pose="explain" id="noise-classifier" label="First convert the classifier’s direction" next="Check the limitations of estimating directions">
Multiplying the score equation by $-\sigma_t$ gives

$$
\begin{gathered}
\widetilde\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t,c)\\
=\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\\
-w\sigma_t\!\nabla_{x_t}\!\log p_\phi(c\mid x_t,t).
\end{gathered}
$$

Both the minus sign and $\sigma_t$ come from the conversion. Here classifier guidance starts from an **unconditional base**, so $w=0$ means no guidance. CFG instead starts from a conditional answer and uses $w=1$ as its baseline.
</Reveal>

<Talk partner="nori" pose="question" id="classifier-limit" label="Does an accurate judgment imply an accurate direction?" next="Finish this route">
We use the gradient of probability as the image changes. Two terrains can have similar heights but different local slopes. Likewise, accurate class probabilities do not automatically imply accurate input gradients.

The generative model in this approach need not accept $c$; a separate noisy classifier supplies the request. Now suppose our available tool differs: **if the generative model already learned the condition, could its own answers reveal this direction?**

<Extra label="What else should we check about the helper?">
Classification accuracy checks answers. Guidance also uses local changes around an image, so ask whether tiny input perturbations cause stable judgments. A classifier may change its answer sharply under a small perturbation; clean-image accuracy alone does not establish the quality of intermediate input gradients. This motivates examining the direction, without claiming that stability guarantees successful generation. <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.08664">Kawar, Ganz, and Elad</a> study robust classifiers for guidance.
</Extra>
</Talk>

<Reveal partner="mugi" pose="explain" id="dropout" label="One parameter set, two kinds of practice" next="Is learning still needed during generation?">
This preparation is **conditioning dropout**. An empty condition $\varnothing$ means no breed or prompt is specified; **it does not mean “no corgis in the image.”** Keep the image, noise level, and added noise. The network still predicts that noise, but sometimes cannot read the description.

Write the condition actually shown as $\widetilde c$, either the original $c$ or the empty condition. The training loss becomes

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathcal L_{\mathrm{drop}}(\theta)\\
&=\mathbb E_{(x_0,c),t,\epsilon,\widetilde c}
\big[\|\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t,\widetilde c)\\
&\hspace{5em}-\epsilon\|^2\big].
\end{aligned}
$$

The expectation averages over data, times, noise, and the random decision to drop the condition. The target remains $\epsilon$. Dropping conditions only for certain breeds would make empty inputs describe selected data. Data-independent dropout instead practices the overall base answer.

After preparation, sampling uses the same $\theta$ to obtain $s_\theta(x_t,t,c)$ and $s_\theta(x_t,t,\varnothing)$, estimating conditional and unconditional scores. Their difference estimates the request’s extra direction. This requires a model already trained on both inputs; it does not instruct us to retrain an unprepared model now.
</Reveal>

<Choose partner="mugi" pose="question" id="c-baseline" label="Where does strengthening start?">
The model can already generate from a condition. We are willing to lose some variation to try making breed features clearer, so we add more of the difference between its two answers. Denote strength by $w$ and start from the conditional prediction:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\widetilde s_\theta(x_t,t,c)\\
=s_\theta(x_t,t,c)\\
+(w-1)\big[s_\theta(x_t,t,c)\\
-s_\theta(x_t,t,\varnothing)\big].
\end{gathered}
$$

**Which $w$ keeps the original conditional prediction without extra strengthening?**
<Option correct reply="The extra coefficient becomes zero, leaving the original conditional answer. This combination is classifier-free guidance (CFG)." why="This formula starts from the conditional prediction, and the extra coefficient is w−1. Its baseline is w=1.">$w=1$.</Option>
<Option why="At w=0, subtracting the difference from the conditional answer leaves the unconditional answer. Even the original condition’s effect is removed.">$w=0$.</Option>
<Option why="At w=2, an extra copy of the difference is added to the conditional answer. That already strengthens guidance.">$w=2$.</Option>
</Choose>

<Talk partner="mugi" pose="explain" id="cfg-combination" label="We can also start from the unspecified answer" next="Think about what strength changes">
Expanding gives an equivalent expression:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde s_\theta
&=s_\theta^\varnothing+w(s_\theta^c-s_\theta^\varnothing)\\
&=w s_\theta^c+(1-w)s_\theta^\varnothing.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $s_\theta^c$ and $s_\theta^\varnothing$ abbreviate answers for the same $(x_t,t)$, making the coefficients easier to inspect. At $w=0$ the result is unconditional; at $w=1$ it is conditional; $w>1$ extrapolates along the difference beyond the original conditional answer.

<Extra label="Which density corresponds to the stronger score?">
Fix one $t$ and assume exact scores. The combination is the log-gradient of these relative weights:

$$
\begin{gathered}
p_t(x_t)\,p_t(c\mid x_t)^w\\
\propto p_t(x_t)^{1-w}p_t(x_t\mid c)^w.
\end{gathered}
$$

Products become sums under log, powers become coefficients, and normalization independent of $x_t$ differentiates to zero. If the total mass is finite, these weights can be normalized at this time.

But writing such a density at every time does not establish that all of them arise from one forward process. This fixed-time equality alone cannot show that sampling with the combined scores ends exactly at $p_0(x_0\mid c)^w p_0(x_0)^{1-w}$.
</Extra>

<Extra label="Why do some papers use a different baseline number?">
Some papers add $w_{\mathrm{paper}}$ copies of the difference to the conditional answer, writing $(1+w_{\mathrm{paper}})s^c-w_{\mathrm{paper}}s^\varnothing$. Our convention is $w=1+w_{\mathrm{paper}}$. The combination is the same, but the strength is measured from a different baseline. Substitute a number into the actual formula before interpreting it.
</Extra>
</Talk>

<Talk partner="nori" pose="suggest" id="negative-prompt" label="What if the unspecified query also contains a description?" next="Finish this route">
“If we do not want blurry photos, could we replace the empty input with ‘blurry,’ then push from ‘a sharp corgi’ away from ‘blurry’?”

This is the idea of a negative prompt. Write the undesired description as $c_-$ and abbreviate noise predictions for the same $(x_t,t)$ by $\epsilon_\theta^c$ and $\epsilon_\theta^{c_-}$. A combination is

$$
\widetilde\epsilon_\theta
=\epsilon_\theta^{c_-}
+w(\epsilon_\theta^c-\epsilon_\theta^{c_-}).
$$

A new sentence tomorrow may combine words already learned, without retraining sentence by sentence. An unseen condition type, however, does not become learned merely because we can write a formula. Classifier guidance can use a separately prepared helper; CFG relies on the generative model’s own conditioning abilities.

This changes the comparison baseline. The model must also understand the input $c_-$. It does not set “the probability of blurry images” to zero, and it no longer directly has the unconditional-base Bayes interpretation.

<Extra label="What if the model learns Flow Matching velocity?">
Conditional training can also give a velocity model the input $c$. Practice on a known reference path $I_t$, such as $I_t=(1-t)X_0+tX_1$ from noise $X_0$ to data $X_1$, with label $\partial_t I_t=X_1-X_0$. Here $t=0\to1$, unlike diffusion’s denoising toward $t=0$.

The conditioning-dropout loss is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathcal L_{\mathrm{drop}}^{\mathrm{FM}}(\theta)\\
&=\mathbb E\big[\|v_\theta(I_t,t,\widetilde c)\\
&\hspace{4em}-\partial_t I_t\|^2\big].
\end{aligned}
$$

The expectation averages over reference endpoints, times, and whether the condition is dropped; the label remains reference velocity. After learning both inputs, we can combine

$$
\begin{gathered}
\widetilde v_\theta(x,t,c)\\
=v_\theta(x,t,\varnothing)\\
+w\big[v_\theta(x,t,c)\\
-v_\theta(x,t,\varnothing)\big].
\end{gathered}
$$

An ODE solver follows this learned field to obtain a generated trajectory. Common Gaussian affine interpolations have a velocity–score conversion connecting to the intuition above. For a general reference path, however, subtracting two velocities alone does not show that the difference is a classifier log-gradient.
</Extra>
