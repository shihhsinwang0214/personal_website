<Say partner="lumi" pose="explain" id="target" label="Which output proportions do we want?" next="Choose a method route">
Picking the best is reasonable if we want one high-scoring photo. But the user also wants alternatives. Our question is whether repeated outputs approach this reward tilt:

$$
\begin{gathered}
p_R(x_0)=\frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}(x_0)e^{\lambda R(x_0)}}{Z_R},\\
Z_R=\mathbb E_{x_0\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}
\big[e^{\lambda R(x_0)}\big].
\end{gathered}
$$

Here $p_{\mathrm{data}}$ is the base distribution, $\lambda>0$ controls reward influence, and $Z_R$ averages the reward multiplier over base-generated finished images. Assume $0<Z_R<\infty$; dividing by it restores total mass to $1$. Discrete results use probabilities and continuous images use densities. An exact point in the latter need not have a nonzero probability of being sampled.

This target raises high-reward relative weights while retaining base proportions. <Ref to="dma-reward-tilting"/> derived its motivation and optimality. **Today we ask how to sample, without updating the model.** First assume the base sampler exactly produces $p_{\mathrm{data}}$; we will return to actual model error later.
</Say>

<Talk partner="mugi" pose="explain" id="best" label="Keep the highest-scoring image in a batch" next="What do repeated runs reveal?">
“Generate $N$ images and pick the highest score. We can already do that, right?”

<Figure src={candidates} alt="Six independently completed dog photos with different breeds and hat colors. An empty frame on the right will return only one; no winner is selected. These are not denoising steps of one image.">
Now that Mugi has proposed generating more images, consider this scene. The left side contains separate candidates; only one is returned on the right. Every candidate must be finished and scored.
</Figure>

Independently generate $x_0^{(1)},\ldots,x_0^{(N)}$ from the base. Score them, find the highest-scoring index $i^\star$, and return that image:

$$
\begin{gathered}
i^\star=\arg\max_{1\le i\le N}R(x_0^{(i)}),\\
\widehat x_0=x_0^{(i^\star)}.
\end{gathered}
$$

This is **Best-of-$N$**. The $\arg\max$ selects an index; it updates neither pixels nor parameters. Ties can use a fixed or random rule. No reward gradient is needed, and each candidate’s original sampling path is unchanged.

Generate a fresh batch of $N$, select one, and independently repeat the whole run $n$ times to collect $n$ outputs. **$N$ is the candidates compared per run; $n$ is the number of collected outputs.** Increasing $n$ reveals this rule’s proportions, which still need to be checked against $p_R$.
</Talk>

<Think partner="lumi" pose="question" id="t-ratio" label="Candidates already came from the base. What is missing?" review="terminal-rules">
Common base results are already more likely to enter the batch. To represent $p_R$, we need the target’s relative increase over the sampling source. Where the base is positive, simplify

$$
\begin{gathered}
\frac{p_R(x_0)}{p_{\mathrm{data}}(x_0)}\\
=\frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}(x_0)e^{\lambda R(x_0)}}
{Z_R\,p_{\mathrm{data}}(x_0)}.
\end{gathered}
$$

**What cancels? What is shared by every candidate?** If we will divide by the batch’s total weight, must that shared constant be computed first?
</Think>

<Reveal partner="lumi" pose="explain" id="importance" label="Correct candidate contributions toward new proportions" next="Calculate for one batch">
The base cancels, leaving $e^{\lambda R(x_0)}/Z_R$. The shared $Z_R$ cancels when normalizing, so candidate $i$ only needs positive weight

$$
\begin{aligned}
\omega^{(i)}&=e^{\lambda R(x_0^{(i)})},\\
\bar\omega^{(i)}
&=\frac{\omega^{(i)}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^N\omega^{(\ell)}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $\omega^{(i)}$ is a relative multiplier; $\bar\omega^{(i)}$ is the probability of selecting candidate $i$ given this batch. **Candidate frequencies already represent the base; selection supplies the reward multiplier.** We do not multiply by base probability again.

Correcting contributions by the target-to-source ratio is the core of **importance sampling**. Here we perform **importance weighting (IW)**, then sample an index with normalized weights and return its finished image.

<Extra label="To estimate a target average, must we select an image?">
Let $f(x_0)$ be a quantity to average, such as a class indicator, with finite absolute expectation under the target. Multiply and divide the expectation’s integrand by the base density:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_{p_R}[f]
&=\mathbb E_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}
\left[\frac{p_R}{p_{\mathrm{data}}}f\right]\\
&=\frac{\mathbb E_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}[e^{\lambda R}f]}
{\mathbb E_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}[e^{\lambda R}]}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Both expectations average base-generated images. The numerator also multiplies by $f$, and the denominator is $Z_R$. Estimate both means with the same $N$ independent candidates; their factors $1/N$ cancel:

$$
\mathbb E_{p_R}[f]
\approx\sum_{i=1}^N\bar\omega^{(i)}f(x_0^{(i)}).
$$

This is **self-normalized importance sampling**. Compute a weighted average directly, without selecting another image. Finite-sample ratios are generally biased. With independent candidates and the relevant integrability, increasing $N$ makes both sample means converge to their expectations, and their ratio to the target average.
</Extra>
</Reveal>

<MethodPath id="path-smc" back="e-methods" title="Resample drafts: SMC and the full FK derivation">

<Talk partner="lumi" pose="suggest" id="p-estimate" study="draft-plans" label="Lumi: first estimate its eventual score">
“Ask the denoiser for a predicted finished image, then show the evaluator. Or complete the same draft independently several times and average the resulting multipliers. Could we compare drafts before finishing?”

We can try, while separating estimates from outcomes. The current noisy state is $x_t$, its denoised estimate is $\hat x_0(x_t,t)$, and only reaching the endpoint gives $x_0$. In the VP representation, for $\bar\alpha_t>0$,

$$
\hat x_0(x_t,t)
=\frac{x_t-\sigma_t\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}
{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}.
$$

Here $\epsilon_\theta$ is predicted noise and $\sigma_t=\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$ is its amplitude. Subtract predicted noise and divide out the signal scale to get a clean estimate. **This is not an already determined future image.**

Estimating future weight with $e^{\lambda R(\hat x_0)}$ only queries reward, without differentiation. With multiple independent completions, compute each $e^{\lambda R}$ first, then average. Those trial completions also cost generation. Both are intermediate estimates, not guaranteed exact future averages.
</Talk>

<Reveal partner="lumi" pose="explain" id="resample" label="Draw indices, then continue those drafts" next="Connect the intermediate loop">
Each draw selects the third draft with probability $0.7$, giving expected count $3\times0.7=2.1$. The first two have expected counts $0.3,0.6$, but actual counts are integers. One run might draw $(A,C,C)$ or three copies of $C$. These are possible outcomes, **not prescribed copying lists**.

Drawing candidates again by probability is **resampling**. Afterward, each copy receives equal weight $1/3$. Higher-weight drafts have more descendants on average and receive more future computation; the total remains three. Neither model nor scorer is retrained.

For now, $1,2,7$ were assumed incremental multipliers for this segment. We still need to derive how future estimates give those incremental weights.
</Reveal>

<Talk partner="lumi" pose="explain" id="smc" label="Compare again after each segment" next="What if we copy only the champion?">
Call an in-progress candidate a **particle** and keep $M$ simultaneously. Diffusion generates from high noise toward $t=0$. Choose checkpoints $T=t_K>\cdots>t_0=0$, where $K$ counts segments and $x_{t_k}^{(i)}$ is draft $i$ at time $t_k$.

From $t_k$ to $t_{k-1}$, first move each draft with the original reverse sampler: **propagate**. Compute its incremental weight $\omega_{k-1}^{(i)}$: **weight**. Normalize over the batch to get $\bar\omega_{k-1}^{(i)}$, then draw $M$ indices with those probabilities: **resample**.

Repeating “propagate → weight → resample” is the basic operation of **Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC)**. Here every checkpoint resamples, resetting weights to $1/M$. At $t=0$, uniformly select one of the final $M$ images to return. **We change which candidates have descendants, not $\theta$.**
</Talk>

<Derivation label="From ratios along one path to the terminal multiplier">
<DeriveStep title="Compare consecutive states on the same path" goal="Define the multiplier for the segment from t_k to t_{k−1}." tool="Take the ratio of the new estimate to the old one." reason="The new candidate must be paired with its actual predecessor to correct the same path’s cumulative weight.">

$$
\begin{gathered}

\end{gathered}
$$

The numerator is the estimate after the segment, and the denominator is the estimate before it. Dividing by another draft’s old estimate would destroy the matching values needed for cancellation.
</DeriveStep>

$$

<Extra label="How do more particles M differ from more fixed-M runs n?">
Let $f(x_0)$ be a bounded image statistic, such as the indicator of $C$. With fixed checkpoints, correct weights, and the corresponding integrability conditions, SMC consistency states

$$
\begin{gathered}
\frac1M\sum_{i=1}^M f(x_0^{(i)})\\
\xrightarrow[M\to\infty]{\text{in probability}}\\
\mathbb E_{x_0\sim p_R}[f(x_0)].
\end{gathered}
$$

The left side averages $M$ particles in one run; the right side is the target average. “In probability” means the probability of exceeding any fixed positive error tolerance tends to $0$. It does not require independent particles within the run.

Increasing $M$ improves each run’s FK-target approximation. Independently repeating a fixed-$M$ sampler $n$ times only clarifies its output proportions, which may remain biased. This limit also does not repair an incorrectly specified terminal weight.
</Extra>

<Extra label="Must every segment resample?">
No. **Effective sample size (ESS)** describes concentration of normalized weights:

$$
\mathrm{ESS}=\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^M(\bar\omega^{(i)})^2}.
$$

With equal weights, the squared sum is $M/M^2=1/M$, giving ESS $M$. If one weight approaches $1$, the squared sum approaches $1$, giving ESS near $1$. This measures weight concentration, not independent ancestor count.

We can resample only when ESS falls below a threshold. If a segment skips resampling, retain the old weights and multiply by the next incremental potential. Skipping resampling cannot be combined with assuming equal weights at the next segment.
</Extra>
</Say>

<Talk partner="nori" pose="suggest" id="source-target" label="The same start always draws the same image" next="How do source proportions give image proportions?">

<Talk partner="nori" pose="explain" id="chains" label="Try a new start, then decide whether it stays" next="Must a lower score be rejected?">
“We have a source target. Could we propose $z'$ from the current $z$, generate $G(z')$, score it, and decide whether to move there?”

Yes, but always accepting only higher scores again finds high-scoring points rather than specified proportions. A sequence of current and subsequent starts is a **chain**. Acceptance moves to $z'$; rejection stays at $z$. Staying still is also a step.

Using target-preserving Markov transitions to collect samples is **Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)**. First consider a symmetric proposal, equally likely in either direction. The acceptance probability is

$$
\begin{gathered}
P_{\mathrm{accept}}(z,z')\\
=\min\big\{1,\\
\frac{p_Z(z')}{p_Z(z)}\\
\cdot e^{\lambda_j[R(G(z'))-R(G(z))]}\big\}.
\end{gathered}
$$

Here $\lambda_j$ is chain $j$’s reward strength. Normalizing constants cancel. The ratio compares **prior density times reward multiplier**. Draw $U\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$; move to $z'$ if $U<P_{\mathrm{accept}}$, otherwise keep $z$. No reward gradient is needed.

<Extra label="Why does this acceptance probability preserve the target?">
Denote the chain target by $\pi(z)=p_Z^{\lambda_j}(z)$ and proposal density by $a(z'\mid z)$. Symmetry means $a(z'\mid z)=a(z\mid z')$. Forward probability flow is

$$
\begin{gathered}
\pi(z)a(z'\mid z)\\
\cdot\min\{1,\pi(z')/\pi(z)\}\\
=a(z'\mid z)\\
\cdot\min\{\pi(z),\pi(z')\}.
\end{gathered}
$$

Exchanging $z,z'$ gives the same reverse flow by symmetry. Including rejection probability at the current state prevents net target mass from flowing to one side. This equal-flow condition is **detailed balance**, giving target invariance.

An asymmetric proposal needs the additional ratio $a(z\mid z')/a(z'\mid z)$. The prior-and-reward-only version no longer suffices. Target preservation is a transition property; sufficient finite-run exploration is a separate question.
</Extra>
</Talk>

<Say partner="mugi" pose="explain" id="extension" label="Could other drafts refer to the current best?" next="Return to the evaluator’s original limitation">

<Extra label="BoNG: how can candidate differences give a direction?">
At time $t$, retain $M$ states $x_t^{(i)}$, compute their denoised estimates $\hat x_0^{(i)}$, score them, and select

$$
i^\star=\arg\max_{1\le i\le M}R(\hat x_0^{(i)}).
$$

We score **predicted completions**, not endpoint images $x_0$. For candidate $i$, the vector $\hat x_0^{(i^\star)}-\hat x_0^{(i)}$ points from its prediction toward the reference prediction. Kim and colleagues call guidance of other particles by the best candidate **Best-of-$N$ Guidance (BoNG)**. Here we keep $M$ for the intermediate candidate count.

How does that displacement convert to noise prediction? Fix current $x_t^{(i)}$ and use VP denoising:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\hat x_0^{(i)}\\
=\frac{x_t^{(i)}-\sigma_t\epsilon_\theta(x_t^{(i)},t)}
{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}},\\
\widetilde{\hat x}_0^{(i)}-\hat x_0^{(i)}\\
=-\frac{\sigma_t}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}\\
\cdot\big(\widetilde\epsilon_\theta^{(i)}
-\epsilon_\theta(x_t^{(i)},t)\big).
\end{gathered}
$$

Subtracting the two denoised estimates cancels fixed $x_t^{(i)}$. To make the left-hand displacement equal $w_{\mathrm B}(\hat x_0^{(i^\star)}-\hat x_0^{(i)})$, rearrange, assuming $\sigma_t>0$ and $\bar\alpha_t>0$:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\widetilde\epsilon_\theta^{(i)}
=\epsilon_\theta(x_t^{(i)},t)\\
-w_{\mathrm B}\frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}{\sigma_t}
\big(\hat x_0^{(i^\star)}-\hat x_0^{(i)}\big).
\end{gathered}
$$

Here $w_{\mathrm B}\ge0$ controls displacement strength. At $w_{\mathrm B}=0$, the original prediction remains. At $w_{\mathrm B}=1$, the new denoised estimate at the fixed current state equals the reference, **without implying the same next state or final image**. The original sampler must use the combined prediction to advance to a lower noise level.

At each reverse step, estimate clean images, score and choose a reference, modify every prediction, and continue. The best index may change. The reference candidate’s own displacement is zero. Candidate differences supply directions without computing $\nabla R$ or updating $\theta$.

Unlike FK, this has not established terminal weighting equal to $e^{\lambda R(x_0)}$, so it does not directly prove exact tilted sampling. The source note also treats it as an extension. This VP conversion retains $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$; do not mix it with a schedule whose signal amplitude is $1$.
</Extra>

<Extra label="What if the model has not learned the data distribution exactly?">
To examine sampling rules, we assumed a base of $p_{\mathrm{data}}$. If the model actually outputs $p_\theta$, the same terminal weighting approximates the normalized distribution with weights

$$
p_\theta(x_0)e^{\lambda R(x_0)}.
$$

With a finite-step sampler, the base is more precisely its actual output law, including discretization error. More candidates, particles, or chain exploration can improve approximation relative to that base, **without automatically correcting it to $p_{\mathrm{data}}$**.

A result with no base support cannot appear through finite-reward reweighting. Even amplified low-probability regions may remain unseen by finite candidates. Model error, sampling approximation, reward, plausibility, and diversity therefore remain distinct checks.
</Extra>
