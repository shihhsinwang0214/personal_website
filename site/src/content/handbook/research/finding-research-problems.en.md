> Choosing the right problem matters more than solving it well. A brilliant solution to the wrong question is wasted brilliance.

Finding problems is the skill that separates a student from a researcher, so we grow it on purpose. Early on I'll hand you problems; the goal is for you to bring me better ones.

## Where good problems come from

- **Friction while reading.** The sentence that made you say "wait, why?" or "that can't be the whole story" is a lead. Keep a running list of these.
- **The gap between claim and reality.** A method that should work but doesn't, or a result that's true but unexplained.
- **Your own itch.** A tool you wish existed; a question a paper raised but didn't answer.
- **Simplify something hard.** Many strong papers are "here's a much simpler way to see X."

## Test a problem before committing

Ask: *Is it important? Is it tractable with what we have? Is it interesting to me? What's the smallest version I could try this week?* A good problem is important **and** approachable — reach for the overlap.

## Keep an ideas list

Write down every half-formed idea, dated. Most won't survive; a few will grow. Revisit the list monthly. Bring your top one or two to our meetings — even rough — and we'll pressure-test them together.

## In short

Collect friction, chase the "wait, why?", and test for important-and-tractable. Owning your problem selection is the milestone that makes you a researcher.
