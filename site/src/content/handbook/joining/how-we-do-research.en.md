> For students and postdocs who may join — or have already joined — the lab, and for people who want to collaborate with us.

This is about **how** we do research: what we believe, how we operate day to day, what we promise you, and what we expect you to do.

## 1. Six things we believe

### 1. Most ideas won't work — by design, not by accident

We work on things nobody has done before. Here, **a failed experiment is a work product, not a failure at work**.

That sentence only counts if we back it up institutionally, so:

- We keep a shared failure log. What was tried, what didn't work, and why — it all goes in.
- Group meetings reserve time for negative results, discussed as seriously as positive ones.

### 2. We prefer fewer and deeper

Three or four incremental papers a year are worth less than one that people will still read in ten years.

This is not about doing less — it's about putting the effort you save into harder problems.
"Will anyone still be reading this in five years?" is a question we actually ask when choosing problems.

### 3. A problem worth doing must be both important and attackable

If a problem is important but you have no idea how to start, it isn't your problem yet.
Of course, we also try to avoid problems you already know exactly how to solve — those may not be important enough.

We look for the boundary. For every ongoing project, we check together:

> Is this representative of a whole class of problems?
> What is our attack?
> Who will cite it in five years? (Can you name specific people or communities?)

If a project can't answer these, we decide together whether to shut it down. Shutting a project down is not punishment — it gives the time back to you and to better, more impactful work.

### 4. Being wrong fast beats being right slowly

In the kind of research we do, the most expensive cost is not compute — it's **the time it takes to find out you're wrong**.

So our default: any new idea runs first in the smallest possible setting, with a first signal within 48 hours.
Toy experiments are not practice — they are the primary weapon. (See Section 3 for the concrete ladder.)

### 5. Keep the door open

In the short term, people who work behind closed doors produce more.
In the long term, people who keep the door open are the ones who know which problems are worth doing.

So we encourage you to attend talks outside your field, grab coffee with people from other areas, and talk about work. This seemingly inefficient time is where our problem selection quality and research progress come from.

### 6. The quality of an idea has nothing to do with the seniority of whoever proposed it

We deliberately keep the team flat. Empirically, research teams with heavy hierarchy produce less novel work,
and they sacrifice the growth of junior members. We don't want that.

Concretely: discussions start with the most junior person; and every year, everyone should aim to have at least one project that *you* lead, not just assist on.

## 2. How we discuss

This is the thing we care about most, so it gets extra detail.

### Challenge ideas, not people

We question each other's results hard — that's what research should look like.
But the question is "why might this method or conclusion have a problem," never "how could you think that."
We make sure the questioning stays about the work, not the person, and we help each other find the possible holes in a presentation or a result.

### "I don't know" beats bluffing — and proposing a next step is a perfect answer

When asked something you don't know, saying "I don't know, but I'll find out" is a hundred times more valuable than bluffing. I say it myself, and I say it often. Even better is having the next step already in mind — whether a concrete action, or the judgment that the direction is wrong and a new angle is needed. Either is an excellent next step.

Also: if you notice I've said something wrong, say so on the spot. On the spot is far more useful than afterward.

### Junior people give it a go first

In group discussions, we encourage the most junior person to start, so that everyone gets used to exercising independent, critical thinking often, right from the beginning. Being wrong is fine — here, silence is the only thing that's harder to accept.

### Criticism mostly in private; in public, recognition or gentle suggestions

Harsher criticism of an individual's work happens in our one-on-ones.
In public, I only ask questions, give recognition, or offer suggestions gently.

## 3. How the day-to-day works

### Group meeting: not a progress report

Our group meeting is not "what I did this week." It's these three questions:

> What did I **learn** this week?
> What **failed**, and why?
> Where am I **stuck** right now?

Being stuck is not embarrassing — it's the reason this meeting exists. Being stuck for more than two days *without saying so* is the problem.

### One-on-ones: that's your meeting

We meet one-on-one regularly. The frequency depends on your familiarity with **the task at hand** —
not on your seniority. The same person might check in every few weeks when running familiar experiments,
and every few days when writing their first rebuttal. If you feel you need an extra session, just say so. My role is to listen, give context, and clear obstacles.
If our one-on-ones turn into me issuing instructions one-way, that is not a good sign.

### The debugging ladder: no skipping levels

```
Level 1  Minimal toy experiment              — hours to a day
Level 2  Small-scale real task               — days
Level 3  Real scientific problem + external validation — weeks
```

Most ideas will probably stop at Level 1. That's a good thing.
"Ran a big experiment for two weeks before discovering the direction was wrong" is the failure mode we most want to avoid.

### Write one page before you start

Before starting any new direction, write these five lines for yourself (and for me):

```
Hypothesis:
Why existing methods can't do this:
Minimal verifiable experiment (toy experiment):
If it fails, what does that mean:
If it succeeds, what's next:
```

The value is in the writing more than the reading. Three months later, you'll be glad you have a stack of these pages.

### Code and reproducibility

One repo, one training framework, one fixed set of baselines.
Not for tidiness — so that your next idea can be tested in a day instead of a week.
High-impact work is built on others being able to use, reproduce, and extend it — people have to be able to run your stuff. That's also why we care about open source.

## 4. Authorship and credit

- Before any paper is submitted, there must be consensus on the contribution breakdown.
- **Everyone gets at least one paper in a leading role** — that's my responsibility to your career.
- Whoever did the main work goes first. I don't adjust the order for seniority.
- If you think an ordering was unfair, say so directly. That conversation won't damage our relationship — avoiding it will.
- Open-source tools, datasets, benchmarks — here, these count as research output, and they go into recommendation letters.

## 5. What I promise you

- I will tell you what I really think, including things you don't want to hear. Tactful, but never vague.
- I will not be unreachable when you need me. Come find me when you're stuck — don't wait for the next meeting. And when you don't need me, you're welcome to cancel early. :)
- Your growth comes before my output. If a project is good for a paper but not for your development, we will talk seriously about how to arrange things.
- I will do my best to protect your time, so you don't have to deal with admin and chores that aren't yours.
- When you leave, you should be able to independently choose problems and independently judge whether a piece of research — a result — is credible. That matters more than how many papers you published.

## 6. What I expect from you

- **Communicate bad news proactively.** Good news can wait three days; bad news gets said the same day.
- **Own your results.** Before showing anything to others, pick it apart yourself first.
- **Respect shared resources.** Compute, data, other people's time.
- **Help those more junior than you.** Everyone here has been helped by someone more senior — including me.
- **Take care of your body and mind.** The pace of this field can make all-nighters look like a virtue. They aren't. Two years of steady output beats three months of burning bright and burning out. When you're on vacation, be on vacation — I won't message you.

## 7. For collaborators

If you're a researcher from another field considering working with us, here is how we collaborate:

**What we can offer**

- Depth on the methods side, and the ability to reformulate your problem into a computable form
- Open-source, reproducible implementations — not models that exist only in the paper
- An honest statement of capability boundaries — we will tell you plainly what can't be done yet

**What we need from you**

- A problem whose answer you **actually care about** — not a problem that merely happens to have data
- A definition of "what counts as a good result." We must settle this before starting, or six months in we'll discover we've been chasing different things
- A point of contact willing to join discussions regularly. A collaboration cannot run on one meeting per quarter

**Timeline realities**

- Methods research moves slower than applied research, with higher uncertainty. We won't make promises we can't keep.
- The first meaningful result usually takes 3–6 months, and the first two months mostly go into defining the problem clearly — that stretch looks unproductive, but it determines everything after.

**Authorship**

- Settle authorship and data usage before the collaboration starts, in writing. This isn't distrust — it's protection for both sides.

## 8. Your first month

| When | What |
|---|---|
| Week 1 | Read this document carefully; read the failure log; have a 30-minute chat with every member |
| Week 2 | Set up your environment and reproduce an existing result — one of the team's is fine. The goal is not novelty; it's walking the full path once |
| Week 3 | Pick a small problem or direction you find interesting and run Level 1 |
| Week 4 | Present at group meeting — **including the parts you messed up**. This is how we get to know you |

Nobody expects new results from you in your first month. Relax.

## 9. Honestly, who this place may not suit

- People who want explicit instructions at every step. We give context, not steps.
- People who can't work in uncertainty for three months. Research often goes long stretches with no visible progress.
- People who see admitting mistakes as weakness. Our entire way of operating is built on the opposite assumption.

If reading these makes you uneasy, that doesn't mean you don't fit — most people feel that way at first.
But it's worth talking with me before you join.
