> For students and postdocs who may join — or have already joined — the lab (and for reference for people who want to collaborate with us).

Here I've laid out some of our research style: what I believe, how the day-to-day works, what I promise students, and what I expect students to do.

## 1. Six things I believe

### 1. Most ideas won't work — but we're not afraid to try, fail, and debug

What we work on is often something nobody has attempted or done before. So here, **a failed short-term result is a work product, not a total failure**.

We hit walls often, but we learn together how to get past them:

- We keep a shared failure log. The ideas we tried, why they didn't work, and how we got past the wall — it all goes in, so that others can dodge the dead ends earlier, or climb over them, when they hit something similar.
- Group meetings reserve time for failed results, so we can seriously discuss the causes and the fixes together.

### 2. Use toy examples to attack existing methods, and to give new ideas a fast debugging path

I believe the most expensive cost is not how much compute you spent — it's **how much time you spent before finding out the method was wrong**.

So my habit is: for any new idea, run it first on a small-scale toy example, and see whether it behaves the way you expected.

We start from the point where existing methods do poorly, and design a toy example that they can't solve well. That way, once our new idea passes it, we have some confidence and experience with the idea — and a basis for finding suitable benchmarks that show the new method's advantages in realistic settings.

### 3. A problem worth doing must be both important and attackable

An important problem you have no idea how to start on may not be your problem yet.
Of course, we also try to avoid a problem you already know exactly how to solve — that one may not be important enough, or may be too easy.

We look for the balance point. We'll walk through these together:

> Is this an important, worthwhile problem? Is it representative of a whole class of problems? Is it broadly applicable? Or does it genuinely solve a specific field's pain point?

> What are our advantages, tools, and ideas?

> Roughly how long will it take to finish? How do we set a stop-loss point?

> Who will cite it in five years? (Can you name a specific community?)

For a project that can't answer these, we can decide whether to shut it down. Shutting a project down is not a fatal loss — it gives the time back to us to do better, more impactful work.

### 4. We prefer fewer and deeper

Three or four papers a year that nobody reads are worth less than one paper that people will still read in ten years.

This is not about doing less — it's about putting the effort you save into more meaningful, harder problems.
"Will anyone still be reading this in five or ten years?" is a question we actually weigh when choosing problems.
I'll also give input on a topic's potential as early as the ideation stage, offering my own view to help everyone decide whether it's worth putting time and energy into the research in front of us.

### 5. Build intuitive, everyday thinking, and learn to connect different experiences

When I do research, what I value most is using intuition and everyday examples to understand and think about problems. Because no matter how complex a theory is, if you can translate it into a familiar everyday situation, it becomes far easier to build a solid, clear understanding — and easier to discover the true essence of the problem. This way of thinking not only helps you build clear research intuition, it also lets you explain your ideas to others in plain, accessible terms.

More importantly, we don't stop at the intuitive level. We learn how to gradually abstract, from these everyday examples, the underlying mathematical models, engineering problems, and scientific principles — and to build connections across different fields. When you can move comfortably back and forth between intuition and abstraction, it becomes much easier to apply the experience you've accumulated to entirely new research problems, shaping and strengthening your capacity for cross-disciplinary thinking and innovation.

### 6. Discuss more, express more — the quality of an idea has nothing to do with seniority

In the short term, people who work behind closed doors may produce more.

But in the long term, the people willing to discuss and articulate their own ideas are the ones who come to know which problems are worth doing — and who, in the process of discussing and persuading others, find their own blind spots and spark better ideas. Everyone's suggestions, regardless of seniority, deserve to be seen and respected.

I'll encourage you to attend courses or talks outside your field, have coffee chats with people from other areas, and discuss and exchange your work. This seemingly inefficient time is a major source of our problem-selection quality and of pushing research forward.

## 2. How we discuss

This is the thing I care about most.

### Challenge ideas, not people

We question each other's results hard — that's what research should look like.
But the question is "why might this method or conclusion have a problem," never "how could you think that."
We make sure the questioning stays about the work, not the person, and we help each other find the possible holes in a presentation or a result.

### "I don't know" beats bluffing — being able to propose a next step is best

When asked something you don't know, you can say "I'm not sure, but I'll go find out quickly" — that's a hundred times more valuable than bluffing. I say it myself (and used to say it often). Even better is having the next step already in mind — whether a concrete action, or the judgment that the direction is wrong and a new angle is needed. Either is an excellent next step.

Also: if you notice I've said something wrong, say so on the spot. On the spot is far more useful than afterward.

### Newcomers, please speak up

In group discussions, we encourage the most junior person to start, so that everyone gets used, from the very beginning, to exercising independent and critical thinking often in discussion — and to practicing the intuitive thinking I described above. Being wrong is fine — that's how we find out where an understanding went off track. Here, the only thing that's harder to accept is staying silent (unless you're feeling unwell).

### Criticism mostly in private; in public, recognition or gentle suggestions

Harsher criticism of an individual's work happens in our one-on-ones.
In public, I only ask questions, give recognition, or offer suggestions gently.

## 3. How the day-to-day works

### Group meeting

Our group meeting is not just a flat account of "what I did this week." Instead, you talk about:

> (Motivation) What am I looking at this week? What problem am I trying to solve? Why is this problem important?

> (Limitation) What problem did I find that isn't accounted for — what situation is being missed? What toy example did I design to illustrate it?

> (Solution) What method did I try? Why did I think of it this way?

> (Progress) What **failed**, where am I **stuck** right now, and why?

Being stuck at a wall is not embarrassing — it's the reason this meeting exists. Being stuck for a long time *without saying so* is the problem that can't be solved. Even when you're assigned to present a paper, you can present it with the same logic.

### One-on-ones

We meet one-on-one regularly, basically on a weekly cadence. But the actual frequency depends on your situation with **the task at hand**. The same person can check in every few weeks when running familiar experiments, but might need to talk every few days when writing a rebuttal for the first time. If you feel you need an extra one-on-one, you can email me anytime; likewise, if you feel you're not ready and want to cancel, please tell me in advance.

My role is to listen to your understanding and how you express it, give you big-picture direction and context, and clear possible obstacles as early as I can — the work stays mainly in your hands. If a discussion turns into me just issuing instructions one-way, that is not a good sign.

### The debugging ladder: please don't skip steps

```
Level 1  Minimal toy experiment              — hours to a day
Level 2  Small-scale real task               — days
Level 3  Real scientific problem + external validation — weeks
```

Most ideas will probably be eliminated at Level 1, or sent back to be reforged from scratch.
Also, the times here are only a rough guide — designing a toy example for the first time can take a lot of time too.

"Ran a big experiment for two weeks before discovering the direction was fundamentally wrong" is the failure mode we most want to avoid.

### Write one page before you start

Before starting any new direction, write these five lines for yourself (and for me):

```
Hypothesis:
Why existing methods can't do this:
Minimal verifiable experiment (toy experiment):
If it fails, what does that mean:
If it succeeds, what's next:
```

The value is in the writing more than the reading — it leaves you a precious record of context and thinking.

### Open-source code and reproducibility

A clearly labeled, well-documented repo isn't for tidiness — it's so that your next idea or experiment can be finished in less time.

At the same time, high-impact work is built on others being able to use, reproduce, and extend it — your work is only useful if people can actually run it. That's also why we care so much about open source.

## 4. Authorship and research contribution

The following will be strictly enforced:

* Before any paper is submitted, we discuss each member's actual contribution thoroughly and reach consensus on the author ordering.
* I want every student, over their research career, to have at least one paper as a **primary contributor (first author or co-first author)**. This isn't just about paper count — it's my responsibility to your career development.
* Author ordering follows **actual research contribution** as the sole principle. Whoever did the main work and drove the research should be listed first. I won't adjust the order for seniority, a "senior/junior" system, or other factors, and I won't list anyone without real contribution as an author.
* Co-first authorship should mean both parties made sufficient and indispensable contributions — not a compromise or a consolation arrangement.
* If you think there's anything unfair about an author ordering, please raise it directly. I believe honest, rational discussion won't hurt our relationship; what really causes misunderstanding is choosing silence.
* I also value research output beyond papers. High-quality open-source tools, datasets, benchmarks, software systems, and research infrastructure are all valuable academic contributions, and they'll be clearly described in recommendation letters.

## 5. What I promise you

- Although the choice is mostly still yours, I'll be strict about quality — assessing a topic's or idea's potential early, so you don't waste time on something with no value.
- I will tell you what I really think, including things you don't want to hear — possibly tactful, but never vague.
- I will not be unreachable when you need me. Come find me when you're stuck — don't wait for the next meeting. And when you don't need me, you're welcome to cancel early. :)
- Your growth comes before my output. If a project is good for producing a paper but not for your development, we will talk seriously about how to arrange things.
- I will do my best to protect your time, so you don't have to deal with admin and chores that aren't yours.
- When you leave, you should be able to independently choose problems and independently judge whether a piece of research — a result — is credible. That matters more than how many papers you published.

## 6. What I expect from you

- **Communicate good and bad news proactively.** Whether the news is good or bad, let me know on Slack. I might reply a bit late, but I'll keep it in mind.
- **Own your results.** Before showing a result to others, pick it apart yourself first.
- **Respect shared resources.** Compute, data, other people's time.
- **Help those more junior than you.** Everyone here has been helped by someone more senior — including me.
- **Take care of your body and mind.** The pace of this field can make all-nighters look like a virtue, but they aren't. Sustained, steady output beats two or three months of relentless burning. And when you want a break, take it properly — I won't message you (I take breaks too).

## 7. For collaborators

If you're a researcher from another field considering working with us, here is how we collaborate:

**What we can offer**

- Depth on the methods side, and the ability to reformulate your problem into a computable form
- Open-source, reproducible implementations — not models that exist only in the paper
- An honest statement of capability boundaries — we will tell you plainly what can't be done yet

**What we need from you**

- A problem whose solution you **actually care about** — not a problem that merely happens to have data
- A definition of "what counts as a good result." We must settle this before starting, or six months in we'll discover we've been chasing different things
- A point of contact willing to join discussions regularly. A collaboration cannot run on one meeting per quarter

**Timeline realities**

- Methods research moves slower than applied research, with higher uncertainty. We won't make promises we can't keep.
- The first meaningful real-world result usually takes 3–6 months, and the first two months mostly go into defining the problem clearly and trying small-scale tests — that stretch looks unproductive, but it determines everything after.

**Authorship**

- Settle authorship and data usage before the collaboration starts, in writing. This isn't distrust — it's protection for both sides.

## 8. A newcomer's first month (suggested)

| When | What |
|---|---|
| Week 1 | Read this document carefully; look through the failure log; have a 30-minute chat with every member |
| Week 2 | Set up your environment and reproduce an existing result, explaining it in your own understanding. The goal is not novelty; it's walking the full path once |
| Week 3 | Pick a small problem or direction you find interesting and run Level 1 |
| Week 4 | Present at group meeting — **including the parts you messed up**. This is how we grow |

Don't worry — nobody expects you to produce some amazing new result in your first month.

## 9. Honestly, who this place may not suit

- People who want very explicit instructions at every step. I'll give you a starting point and context, but not the detailed route.
- People who can't work in uncertainty for three months. Research often goes long stretches with no visible progress (even if our toy examples give a little bit of joy).
- People who see admitting mistakes as weakness — our entire way of operating is built on the opposite assumption.

If reading these makes you quite uneasy, that doesn't mean you don't fit at all — most people feel that way at first.

You can choose another lab, or talk with me before joining.
