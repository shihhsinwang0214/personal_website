> This page is not a recruitment ad. It's an honest description of what research here is really like, written to help you decide — including deciding that somewhere else fits you better.

Choosing a lab is one of the most consequential decisions of your early career, and it's usually made with too little information. So instead of selling you on the lab, I want to describe it plainly: how the work actually feels, what I expect, how I mentor, and what people commonly get wrong about research. If, after reading, you feel a quiet "yes" — wonderful. If you feel "this isn't me" — that's an equally valuable outcome, and knowing it now saves us both years.

## What research actually feels like

Research is not a steady march from question to answer. Most days, things don't work. You'll spend a week chasing an idea that dissolves, fix one problem to reveal three more, and reread the same paper four times before it clicks. Progress is real but uneven — long flat stretches punctuated by sudden clarity.

The reward is not constant validation. It's the specific joy of *understanding something that was previously confusing*, and occasionally, understanding something that few people have understood before. If that joy is enough to carry you through the flat stretches, research will suit you. If you mostly enjoy the feeling of completing well-defined tasks, you may find research frustrating — and that's not a flaw, just a fit.

## The culture here

We care about **research culture over rules**. I'd rather share principles you can apply in new situations than hand you a rulebook. The four things we return to constantly are **curiosity, ownership, integrity, and persistence** (more in [Lab Philosophy](/personal_website/handbook/lab-philosophy)). We're kind to people and tough on ideas: you can be wrong, ask "basic" questions, and disagree with me without any cost to how you're treated.

The goal is not for you to complete my tasks. It's for you to grow into someone who finds and frames good problems yourself. That means the environment rewards **independent thinking over task completion** — which is liberating for some and unsettling for others.

## How I mentor, and what I expect

Early on I'm hands-on; over time I deliberately step back so that independence becomes *your* skill (see [Mentoring Philosophy](/personal_website/handbook/mentoring-philosophy)). I optimize for your growth, not just the next paper. In return I ask for ownership, honesty, and early communication — especially "I'm stuck." Expectations run both ways; the full version is in [Expectations](/personal_website/handbook/expectations).

## Common misconceptions about research

- **"You have to be the smartest person in the room."** No. Persistence, taste, and honesty matter more than raw speed. The people who do great work are usually the ones who keep going.
- **"Research is linear."** It isn't. Dead ends are the normal texture of the work, not evidence you're failing.
- **"It's a solitary genius activity."** Rarely. Most good work involves discussion, feedback, and standing on others' shoulders.
- **"Failure means you're not cut out for it."** Failed experiments are data, not verdicts. Learning to be productively wrong is the job.
- **"You need to already know everything."** You don't. The skill is learning what you're missing, fast — not having memorized it.
- **"Publications are the point."** They're a byproduct of understanding something real. Optimize for the understanding.

## Who tends to thrive here — and who might be happier elsewhere

People tend to thrive here if they're pulled by curiosity, take ownership without being pushed, and can sit with not-knowing long enough to make progress. People are often happier in a different environment if they prefer clearly-defined tasks with known answers, want day-to-day instructions more than a direction to explore, or lose energy once a problem stops being easy. None of these are character flaws — they're preferences, and matching them to the right environment is what makes people flourish.

## A note on doubt

Almost everyone who belongs in research also, at some point, feels they don't. Impostor feelings are common and are not a reliable signal about your fit. The more reliable signals are the ones this page has been describing: what energizes you, how you respond to being stuck, and whether the joy of understanding is enough.
