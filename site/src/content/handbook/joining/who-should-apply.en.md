> If you are curious, take ownership, and are willing to be wrong on the way to being right, you'll fit in here.

I care much more about *how you think* than about what you already know. Backgrounds vary — math, CS, physics, biology, engineering. What the people who do well here share is a set of habits, not a checklist of courses.

## You'll probably thrive here if you

- Get genuinely curious about *why* something works, not just whether it works.
- Like to take a vague problem and make it precise yourself, before asking what to do next.
- Treat being wrong as information, not failure — and change your mind when the evidence says so.
- Finish things: you push an idea past the first obstacle instead of drifting to a new one.
- Care about doing honest, careful work even when nobody is checking.

## You might not (yet) enjoy it if

- You prefer well-defined tasks with a known answer and a clear rubric.
- You want day-to-day instructions more than a direction to explore.
- You lose interest once a problem stops being easy.

None of these are permanent traits — they're preferences, and they can change. But it's worth being honest with yourself about what kind of work energizes you.

## Background and prerequisites

No single background is required — math, CS, physics, and engineering are all fine starting points. But I want to be honest: the research here is fairly math-heavy. You do **not** need to already know the tools we use; what I actually need is that you're *willing to learn them and able to*.

So you can calibrate what "able to" means, here are a few mathematical tools at roughly the difficulty level of our day-to-day work:

- Eigendecomposition and the SVD — what a matrix is doing, and why it can be factored that way.
- The multivariable chain rule and the Jacobian.
- Conditional expectation — what it is, and why it's the "best guess."
- Change of variables for probability densities.
- What a simple ordinary differential equation is describing.

You don't need to have mastered all five. If the list makes you think "give me time and I can learn this" — the bar is not a problem. If it makes you think "I never want to touch this" — that's honest, useful information; don't ignore it.

One more thing, so it doesn't surprise you: in an interview I'll usually ask you to pick **one mathematical tool you know well** (not limited to this list) and explain it to me the way you'd teach a classmate. I care about whether you can make an idea clear at least as much as what you already know.

## In short

Curiosity, ownership, and persistence beat a polished CV. If that sounds like you, please [apply](/personal_website/handbook/application-process).
