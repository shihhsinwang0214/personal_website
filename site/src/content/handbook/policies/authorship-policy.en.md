> Credit should track contribution, and the conversation should happen early — not the night before submission.

Authorship is one of the few things that can quietly sour a lab, so we make it explicit and discuss it up front.

## Who is an author

Authorship reflects **substantial intellectual contribution** to the work — ideas, design, key implementation, analysis, or writing — combined with accountability for the result. Roughly, an author can explain and stand behind the part they contributed. We follow the norms of our field and venues, and we err toward being generous and fair.

## Order

- **First author:** the person who drove the project — usually the main ideas, experiments, and writing. Co-first authorship (with a footnote) is used when contributions are genuinely comparable.
- **Middle authors:** meaningful contributions, ordered by contribution where that's customary.
- **Last/senior author:** the advisor, providing direction, funding, and oversight.

## How we decide

We talk about likely roles **early**, revisit as the project evolves, and confirm before submission. Doing real work earns real credit; a title or seniority alone does not. If you contributed and you're unsure where you stand, ask — early and directly. I'd always rather have that conversation sooner.

## Acknowledgements

Help that doesn't meet the authorship bar — a useful discussion, compute, feedback on a draft — is credited generously in acknowledgements.

## If there's a disagreement

Raise it with me. We resolve authorship by discussing actual contributions, openly and kindly, guided by fairness rather than status.

## In short

Credit follows contribution, first author is the person who drove it, and we discuss roles early and revisit often. When unsure, ask.
