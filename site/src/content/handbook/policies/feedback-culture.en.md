> Feedback is a gift of attention. We're tough on ideas because we respect the person.

A lab lives or dies on how it handles feedback. Ours rests on one separation: **we critique the work, never the worth of the person.** Once that's trusted, we can be direct without anyone feeling diminished.

## How we give feedback

- **Direct and specific.** Vague praise and vague criticism both waste the moment. Point at the thing.
- **On the work, not the person.** "This argument has a gap" — not "you're careless."
- **Timely and frequent.** Small, regular feedback beats a rare, overwhelming verdict.
- **Balanced and honest.** Name what's working and what isn't; don't sugarcoat the important problem.
- **Actionable.** End with what you'd try next.

## How we receive feedback

- **Assume good intent.** Critique of your draft is an investment in it, not an attack on you.
- **Listen before defending.** Understand the point first; you can disagree after.
- **Separate your work from your ego.** Your paper being wrong doesn't make you less capable — it makes your next paper better.
- **Say thanks and follow up.** Feedback ignored stops arriving.

## Upward feedback

Please give *me* feedback too — on mentoring, meetings, workload. I mean it: I can only fix what I know about, and a lab that only critiques downward isn't healthy.

## In short

Critique the work, respect the person, keep it specific and frequent, and assume good intent. Feedback flows in every direction here, including toward me.
