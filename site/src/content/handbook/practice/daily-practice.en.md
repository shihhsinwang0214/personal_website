> This page is the day-to-day mechanics: how we meet, how we push an idea forward, how we write code. The beliefs behind it are in [Six Things I Believe](/personal_website/handbook/research-principles).

## Group meeting

Our group meeting is not just a flat account of "what I did this week." Instead, you talk about:

> (Motivation) What am I looking at this week? What problem am I trying to solve? Why is this problem important?

> (Limitation) What problem did I find that isn't accounted for — what situation is being missed? What toy example did I design to illustrate it?

> (Solution) What method did I try? Why did I think of it this way?

> (Progress) What **failed**, where am I **stuck** right now, and why?

Being stuck at a wall is not embarrassing — it's the reason this meeting exists. Being stuck for a long time *without saying so* is the problem that can't be solved. Even when you're assigned to present a paper, you can present it with the same logic.

## One-on-ones

We meet one-on-one regularly, basically on a weekly cadence. But the actual frequency depends on your situation with **the task at hand**. The same person can check in every few weeks when running familiar experiments, but might need to talk every few days when writing a rebuttal for the first time. If you feel you need an extra one-on-one, you can email me anytime; likewise, if you feel you're not ready and want to cancel, please tell me in advance.

My role is to listen to your understanding and how you express it, give you big-picture direction and context, and clear possible obstacles as early as I can — the work stays mainly in your hands. If a discussion turns into me just issuing instructions one-way, that is not a good sign.

## The debugging ladder: please don't skip steps

```
Level 1  Minimal toy experiment              — hours to a day
Level 2  Small-scale real task               — days
Level 3  Real scientific problem + external validation — weeks
```

Most ideas will probably be eliminated at Level 1, or sent back to be reforged from scratch.
Also, the times here are only a rough guide — designing a toy example for the first time can take a lot of time too.

"Ran a big experiment for two weeks before discovering the direction was fundamentally wrong" is the failure mode we most want to avoid.

## Write one page before you start

Before starting any new direction, write these five lines for yourself (and for me):

```
Hypothesis:
Why existing methods can't do this:
Minimal verifiable experiment (toy experiment):
If it fails, what does that mean:
If it succeeds, what's next:
```

The value is in the writing more than the reading — it leaves you a precious record of context and thinking.

## Open-source code and reproducibility

A clearly labeled, well-documented repo isn't for tidiness — it's so that your next idea or experiment can be finished in less time.

At the same time, high-impact work is built on others being able to use, reproduce, and extend it — your work is only useful if people can actually run it. That's also why we care so much about open source.
