> Using AI to think faster is good; using it to avoid thinking ends up hurting you. You are always accountable for what carries your name.

AI tools (chat assistants, coding copilots, literature helpers) are genuinely useful and welcome here. The line is simple: **its output is a reference to be verified, not a conclusion.** What you finally hand in must faithfully reflect your own experience, understanding, and judgment.

## Good uses

- Explaining an unfamiliar concept or paper at your level, as a starting point.
- Boilerplate code, refactors, tests, and rubber-ducking a bug.
- Drafting, tightening, or translating prose you then own and check.
- Brainstorming candidate directions or hypotheses that you then evaluate critically.
- Collaborating on mathematical derivations. This is exactly why I want you to at least be able to "read" math — you need to be able to judge whether each step it gives is right, rather than taking it on faith.

## Use with care

- **Never trust facts, citations, math, or results without verifying them.** Models fabricate confidently. A citation you didn't read doesn't exist as far as we're concerned.
- **Don't let AI author claims you can't defend.** If you can't explain it without the tool, you don't understand it yet — and in group meeting or a one-on-one, I'll likely ask you about exactly that step.
- **Different tasks can have different boundaries.** If there's a task I'd rather you did once yourself first (a first derivation, your first read of a certain kind of paper), I'll say so directly. When I haven't said anything, the default is: use it as a learning aid, but don't put unverified content straight into a research product.

## Never paste these into an external AI

This one isn't negotiable, because once it's out you can't take it back:

- Accounts, passwords, API keys, or any other credentials;
- Personal data, restricted data, or anything that could identify a research participant;
- Unpublished papers, private code, internal documents, and research ideas that aren't cleared for sharing (including papers you're reviewing);
- Anything a contract, licence, or collaboration agreement forbids sharing.

## Note it briefly in your records

In your experiment log or report, say in a sentence or two: where AI was used, what it affected, and what you verified yourself. You don't need to keep the full chat transcript, unless that transcript is itself the evidence.

When AI use is material to a result or a paper, disclose it as the venue requires.

## What you should be able to do without AI answering for you

However much you use it, I want you to be able to, at any point:

- Explain the core idea of the method;
- Say why you chose this experiment rather than another;
- Respond to follow-up questions about assumptions, results, and failure conditions;
- Re-judge and adapt the approach when conditions change;
- Reject suggestions that read fluently but have no evidence behind them.

That last one is harder than it sounds. What AI gives you usually reads as reasonable — and telling "reasonable" apart from "supported by evidence" is the core of research training.
