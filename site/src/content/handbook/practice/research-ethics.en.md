> Integrity is the one thing we never trade for a result. A career is long, and trust, once lost, usually doesn't come back.

## The non-negotiables

- **Never fabricate or falsify.** Not data, not results, not citations. Report what happened, including the inconvenient parts.
- **No plagiarism.** Words, code, figures, and ideas get credited to their source. When in doubt, cite.
- **Report honestly.** Include failures, ablations that didn't help, and limitations. Cherry-picking is a form of lying — a failed result is data, it belongs in the failure log, not hidden.
- **Give fair credit.** Actual research contribution is the sole principle; see [Authorship and Research Contribution](/personal_website/handbook/authorship), and acknowledge help and prior work generously.
- **Respect confidentiality.** Papers under review, private data, and others' unpublished ideas stay private — and don't paste them into external AI tools (see [Using AI Tools](/personal_website/handbook/ai-usage)).

## Data, credentials, and resources

These are closer to engineering hygiene, but the cost of getting them wrong is high:

- Credentials (passwords, API keys, tokens) don't go into code, notes, public issues, or chat messages. Use the lab-approved way of managing them.
- Data, models, code, and results live in the agreed locations, following the backup, access, and sharing rules. If your result only exists on your own laptop, it effectively doesn't exist.
- Verify citations against the original source. Check the licence before using someone else's code, models, or data.
- When human participants, personal data, or sensitive data are involved, please don't collect or upload anything before the ethics and consent procedures are confirmed.
- Before a large experiment, settle the compute budget, how you'll monitor it, and under what conditions you stop. That saves resources, and it also stops you spending two weeks before realising the direction was wrong.

## Everyday integrity

Most ethics isn't dramatic — it's the small choices: double-checking a suspicious result, saying "I'm not sure," rerunning something you know you did sloppily. It starts with being honest *with yourself* about what you actually know.

The same holds in discussion: ideas can be corrected in public, but feedback that touches on someone's ability, working habits, or a sensitive situation happens privately (see [How We Discuss](/personal_website/handbook/how-we-discuss)).

## If something feels wrong

Raise it — with me, or through the appropriate university channel if needed. Mistakes made honestly are fine and expected; hiding them is not. You will never be penalised for reporting a genuine error or concern.

You can also just say "I don't know right now." Here it is safe to float half-formed ideas, admit what you don't know, and ask for help; where we are strict is evidence, assumptions, code, derivations, and the quality of what you deliver.
