> This is the thing I care about most.

## Challenge ideas, not people

We question each other's results hard — that's what research should look like.
But the question is "why might this method or conclusion have a problem," never "how could you think that."
We make sure the questioning stays about the work, not the person, and we help each other find the possible holes in a presentation or a result.

## "I don't know" beats bluffing — being able to propose a next step is best

When asked something you don't know, you can say "I'm not sure, but I'll go find out quickly" — that's a hundred times more valuable than bluffing. I say it myself (and used to say it often). Even better is having the next step already in mind — whether a concrete action, or the judgment that the direction is wrong and a new angle is needed. Either is an excellent next step.

Also: if you notice I've said something wrong, say so on the spot. On the spot is far more useful than afterward.

## Newcomers, please speak up

In group discussions, we encourage the most junior person to start, so that everyone gets used, from the very beginning, to exercising independent and critical thinking often in discussion — and to practicing the [intuitive way of thinking](/personal_website/handbook/research-principles) described in the principles. Being wrong is fine — that's how we find out where an understanding went off track. Here, the only thing that's harder to accept is staying silent (unless you're feeling unwell).

## Criticism mostly in private; in public, recognition or gentle suggestions

Harsher criticism of an individual's work happens in our one-on-ones.
In public, I only ask questions, give recognition, or offer suggestions gently.
